## Frame The Question

Before collecting third-party conclusions, identify the decision this research should inform and the tension that makes it non-obvious. Define the unit of analysis, relevant customer task, geography and useful horizon. Clarify consequential boundaries such as component versus complete system, new demand versus replacement, external sales versus customer self-build, and revenue versus profit pool.

Read relevant prior studies and private core notes under `知识来源/`. State the starting view and plausible competing explanations; revise them as evidence arrives. The starting frame is a working hypothesis, not a thesis to defend. Preserve substantive changes in assumptions when revising an existing study.

An explicit deep-research request calls for a substantive investigation with the rigor of a professional analyst team's study. A short public-source scan, plausible narrative or polished summary is insufficient. Scope controls which questions belong in the study; it is not a reason to stop early on the questions that do. Do not claim a month of work, multiple analysts or comprehensive coverage unless those facts are true. Demonstrate depth through the analysis and evidence.

## Design And Conduct The Investigation

### Question Tree And Evidence Map

Decompose the decision into questions whose answers would change the conclusion. Identify dependencies: understanding the mechanism may be necessary before judging cost, which may be necessary before forecasting procurement. Prioritize branches by importance and uncertainty, while covering enough of the system to avoid an attractive but incomplete explanation.

For an industry or technology study, consider the following coverage and explain consequential exclusions. These are research dimensions, not a fixed chapter template:

- **Historical evolution:** what earlier approaches tried, why they won or failed, what changed recently and which constraints remain. Include failed or displaced approaches when they test the current narrative.
- **Technical foundations and routes:** mechanism, evidence of performance, engineering trade-offs, system limits, alternatives and the conditions under which preferences change.
- **Products, users and procurement:** the actual task, buyer and budget, available products, purchase criteria, deployment work, replacement case and repeat purchasing.
- **Value chain and industrial organization:** suppliers, customers, complementors, substitutes, standards, distribution, bargaining power and the location of cost and profit.
- **Competitive and geographic structure:** consequential players and self-build; global and China dynamics when in scope, including differences in customer requirements, capabilities, policy and supply chains. Do not substitute a list of names for comparison or infer one region from the other.
- **Economics, scale and timing:** unit economics, independent demand drivers, supply or adoption constraints, plausible scenarios and what could overturn them.

Keep a compact working map linking each central question to competing explanations, the evidence needed, sources obtained, unresolved contradictions and the effect on the judgment. Use notes or a table as convenient. This is a way to direct research and reveal gaps, not a claim-by-claim ledger or a required appendix. Update it as the investigation changes the question.

### Investigate In Complementary Lanes

Work from the initial view through technical foundations and original literature, product and market facts, then independent derivation. Triangulate these lanes: a paper can explain a mechanism, product evidence can show what has been implemented, and economics can test whether the capability matters enough to buy. None alone establishes the whole thesis.

When tools and scope permit, divide research by expertise: technology and literature; customers and competition; economics and market model; and an independent challenge to the emerging thesis. Give each contributor a concrete question, boundary and required evidence, not merely a report section to fill. Parallelize source work and analysis; keep one lead responsible for shared definitions, integration, contradictions and the final view. Multiple agents agreeing on the same source are not independent corroboration.

Make the handoff inspectable: contributors should return decisive source locations and conditions, relevant calculations or model files, the reasoning from evidence to conclusion, contrary findings and unresolved conflicts. Keep these in compact working notes with the source material. The lead checks the support for central claims and reconciles disagreements rather than stitching together fluent summaries. Use enough detail to permit review without requiring a universal handoff form or duplicating every source.

Read decisive original materials beyond titles, abstracts and search excerpts. Trace important conclusions to the relevant experiment, table, method, filing section or product limitation. Follow citations and conflicting findings when they can change the result. Use [source-and-evidence.md](source-and-evidence.md) for source precision and [market-sizing.md](market-sizing.md) for economic derivation.

### Synthesize, Challenge And Research Again

Do not make research a single collection pass followed by writing. After a substantive first pass, draft the core explanation, compare it with the strongest alternative and inspect the evidence map:

- Which links in the causal chain are supported, inferred or still missing?
- Which important observation does the preferred explanation fail to account for?
- Which apparent consensus depends on repeated use of the same source, a convenient market definition or an untested assumption?
- What targeted reading, calculation, comparison or counterexample would distinguish the explanations most effectively?

Then perform that work and rewrite the argument, model and affected conclusions. Repeat while a consequential contradiction or unsupported inference remains tractable. A generic caveat does not resolve contradictory evidence. If a limit cannot reasonably be resolved, identify the affected conclusion and bound it explicitly; do not move all unfinished analytical work into questions for the user or a founder.

An unknown does not automatically require a contract, customer interview, data request or full audit. Exhaust useful available evidence and inference first; seek additional access only where the remaining uncertainty matters. Preserve unresolved gaps honestly without treating them as permission to deliver a shallow study.

## Build The Explanation

Use the dependencies relevant to the question:

```text
decision question and customer task
  -> how the present system works and where it is constrained
  -> technical or economic change and the mechanism behind it
  -> alternatives, trade-offs and newly binding constraints
  -> customer behavior, procurement and competitive response
  -> revenue, cost, bargaining power and distribution of profit
  -> current judgment, timing and conditions that could reverse it
```

Do not start with a market CAGR and work backward to a thesis. Introduce a market model where it helps size the causal drivers; it does not replace explaining them. Use [technology-and-competition.md](technology-and-competition.md) to explain relevant mechanisms in language the intended reader can follow.

Organize synthesis around the important disagreements, rather than one paragraph per source or an inventory of companies. For each central argument, connect:

- **Observation:** what has changed, with its date, scope and source. Separate observed change, attributed claim and analyst assumption.
- **Mechanism:** why that change would alter a constraint, behavior or economic outcome. Show the intermediate steps; correlation or two simultaneous announcements do not establish the causal link.
- **Competing explanation:** a plausible account of the same observations that would imply a different outcome. Explain what each account predicts, rather than attaching a generic risk paragraph to the preferred view.
- **Current judgment:** which explanation is better supported now, why, how far it applies and where confidence is limited. A useful conclusion can be conditional, but it should still help the reader choose what to believe or do.

These are reasoning moves, not mandatory subsection labels. Put evidence next to the claim it supports and make the important inference explicit. Use concrete examples, modest calculations and diagrams to expose hidden assumptions. Separate data-based charts from conceptual illustrations; visual form must not give a speculative relationship the appearance of measurement.

## Develop Industry Views And Scenarios

A trend is an explanation of changing behavior and economics, not a label such as "platformization," "AI adoption" or "cost reduction." Show how several observations fit together and where they conflict. Follow the important effects far enough to identify who benefits, who loses and why the market may distribute value differently from today.

Useful distinctions include:

- A technical capability becomes possible, becomes economical, becomes easy to adopt and becomes routinely purchased at different times.
- Growth in deployed units can coexist with falling revenue per unit or shrinking supplier margins.
- A supplier winning share does not prove that the total market is growing; an industry growing does not prove that independent suppliers retain the profit.
- A lower entry barrier may create more customers and more competitors simultaneously. Their relative speed, customer segments and distribution access determine which effect dominates.
- A temporary integration bottleneck can support revenue without creating a durable business; explain whether repeated delivery accumulates reusable assets or remains labor-intensive.

Choose the distinctions that decide the report's thesis. Do not insert them mechanically into every sector.

### Timing And Scenarios

Anchor timing in dependencies, incentives and observable milestones. Explain which actor must change behavior, why it would do so, and what must be ready first. Give a date range only with a defensible basis, and distinguish current business, plausible adoption and longer-term option value. A dated roadmap is not evidence that customers will adopt on schedule.

Use scenarios when different paths change the decision. Vary consequential drivers or competing mechanisms, explain how their interaction changes the result, and state which path currently seems more plausible. "Adoption succeeds" versus "adoption is slow" merely renames good and bad outcomes. More useful scenarios might distinguish a common standard expanding specialist sales from the same standard enabling an incumbent to bundle the capability; the report must explain the conditions and evidence favoring each.

State what observation would discriminate between the paths or reverse the current judgment. Keep this proportional: a few decision-changing signals can be enough, and no invented probabilities or precise years are needed. Do not defer all reasoning to future verification when the existing evidence supports a present view.

### Illustrative Synthesis

The following is a fictional reasoning example, not industry evidence or a forecast:

> Suppose a common interface reduces installation work for an industrial software tool from 30 to 5 person-days. More customers can now afford deployment, which supports faster adoption. But the same interface also makes suppliers easier to replace. If buyers mainly value the basic function and incumbents can include it in an existing contract, sales volume can grow while independent suppliers' prices and margins fall. The competing explanation is that lower installation cost makes specialist sales economical and lets them serve a much larger customer base. Which effect dominates depends on the source of their advantage: a replaceable connector, or measurable workflow results that remain valuable after compatibility improves. Under the additional assumption that customers obtain similar results from both products and prefer their existing supplier, the current view would favor adoption while remaining cautious about standalone vendors. Evidence of repeat purchases and sustained pricing for differentiated specialist outcomes would weaken that view.

The useful work is the chain from integration cost to adoption, substitution, purchasing and profit, including why one interpretation is currently preferred. Do not reuse the example's conclusion as a rule for all standardizing markets.

## Review And Complete The Argument

Before delivery, conduct a strong opposing review of the central thesis. When useful and available, use a separate reviewer who sees the evidence, assumptions and draft without being told to defend the author's conclusion. The reviewer should reconstruct the strongest rival explanation, challenge the model and identify observations that the thesis cannot explain. Resolve material findings through evidence or revise the conclusion; do not merely append the review as a risks section.

Keep a brief disposition of material review findings in the existing working map or notes: what was challenged, what evidence or calculation resolved it, and how the argument changed. If a finding remains unresolved, record which conclusion is limited and why. This gives the lead a way to confirm that review changed the work; it need not become another user-facing checklist or track cosmetic comments.

Review the consequential claims as a skeptical reader:

- Can the reader explain the core mechanism and its trade-off without consulting a glossary? Do examples and figures clarify the step on which the conclusion depends?
- Does each major industry view explain why the observations lead to the conclusion, rather than merely restating them?
- Does the preferred interpretation survive the strongest alternative, including substitutes or a customer choosing not to buy?
- Are market boundaries, periods and price bases comparable where the argument relies on a comparison?
- Is the conclusion still informative if the names of companies and technologies are removed? If it reduces to "better products win" or "costs fall and adoption rises," develop the missing causal specifics.
- Does the report distinguish progress in the technology, adoption by customers and durable value captured by a business?
- Does timing follow from dependencies rather than an attractive timeline? Which variable or event could materially change it?
- Has the analysis taken a supported position, or hidden the lack of synthesis behind jargon, balanced lists and a long set of questions for someone else?

Completion depends on substantive coverage of the central questions, explanatory depth, precision of decisive evidence and calculations, testing against counterevidence, and clear implications for action. Confirm that material contradictions have been resolved or their effect on the conclusion is explicitly bounded. A report that fails these tests needs further research or revision even if it is long, well cited and visually polished. Source counts, word counts and claimed analyst-hours are not substitutes for these tests.

The aim is a defensible explanation, not forced contrarianism. Agreement with a common view is useful when the report explains why it is right, where it stops applying and what it implies. Disclose important unanswered questions and what could still be concluded despite them, without expanding the study into unrelated topics.

## Investment Output

Separate the conclusions that answer different decisions:

- **Industry need:** which tasks justify buying the capability, and what expands or limits that demand?
- **Route view:** which mechanism is preferred for which task, and at what trade-off?
- **Value capture:** which layer can retain price and profit as the industry develops, and which attractive technical work may become cheap or internalized?
- **Timing:** what is commercially available now, what adoption depends on, and what remains option value?
- **Deal view, when in scope:** company quality, evidence, valuation, position and rights. A favorable sector view does not settle the deal.

For a thematic report, identify investable directions and unattractive or crowded areas with reasons, then the few milestones that could change that view. Do not end with only an inventory of opportunities and risks. For project-linked work, map findings into the existing project judgment without creating a parallel judgment file.

When an expert interview or thematic material is an input, preserve it once under `知识来源/`. Cite or link its core note and add analysis; do not recreate another transcript or source summary unless a short decision-relevant excerpt is necessary.
